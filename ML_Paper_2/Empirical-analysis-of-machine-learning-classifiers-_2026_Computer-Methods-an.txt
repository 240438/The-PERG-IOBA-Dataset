Pattern Electroretinogram (PERG) signals offer critical insights into the functional status of retinal ganglion cells
and are widely used for diagnosing optic nerve and retinal pathologies. While existing research has largely
focused on binary classification or full-spectrum signal analysis, limited studies have addressed the use of fre­
quency band-based feature extraction in multiclass classification. This study presents a novel methodology that
segments PERG signals into defined frequency bands and extracts a robust set of 33 time-domain features per eye,
encompassing statistical, morphological, and temporal characteristics. The dataset, originally comprising 15
diagnostic classes were consolidated into five clinically relevant pathology categories to enhance class balance
and interpretability. Feature relevance was validated through one-way ANOVA, identifying key discriminative
features (e.g., slope, std) with high F-values and p< 0.001. Several machine learning models were developed and
evaluated, including Multilayer Neural Networks using the Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm (MLNN-LM),
Random Forest, Bagged Trees, RUSBoost, and AdaBoost implemented via an Error-Correcting Output Codes
(ECOC) framework. Among these, the AdaBoost-ECOC model demonstrated superior performance with an
average classification accuracy of 99.11% ± 1.26, precision of 82.92% ± 0.59, and F1-score of 83.03% ± 0.55.
These findings highlight the clinical applicability of band-specific time-domain features, supported by statistical
validation, in enabling accurate and efficient multiclass classification of PERG signals.

1. Introduction
Early detection of eye disorders is critical, yet clinical evaluation can
be time-consuming and may delay diagnosis until symptoms become
severe [1]. With the increasing availability of non-invasive diagnostics
techniques, there is a strong interest in using artificial intelligence (AI)
to support automated and objective eye disease detection [2]. Among
these technologies, ocular electrophysiology offers a direct and quanti­
tative assessment of retinal function by measuring electrical responses
elicited by visual stimuli [3]. Core techniques like flash electroretinog­
raphy (ERG) and visual evoked potentials (VEP) have long been used in
clinical diagnosis. Flash ERG captures photoreceptor (a-wave) and bi­
polar cell (b-wave) activity using full-field stimuli, while VEP assesses

cortical responses and is useful for evaluating optic nerve integrity
[4–8]. Although structural diagnostic tools such as optical coherence
tomography (OCT) and visual field analysis are widely used, they often
detect disease at later stages. In contrast, electrophysiological methods
such as multifocal ERG (mfERG) and multifocal VEP can reveal func­
tional impairments earlier in disease progression [9].
In ophthalmic applications, electroretinography (ERG) and its
variant, pattern electroretinography (PERG), use corneal or skin elec­
trodes to capture retinal responses to visual stimuli [10]. Pattern elec­
troretinography (PERG) is particularly sensitive to early dysfunction of
retinal ganglion cells (RGCs), making it clinically valuable in conditions
such as glaucoma [11]. PERG employs contrast-reversing checkerboard
stimuli focused on the macular region, producing characteristic P50 and

ISCEV-recommended protocols, though the signals remain susceptible to
noise arising from eye movements, fixation instability, and electro­

Recent advances in signal processing and machine learning have
significantly enhanced the analytical potential of PERG signals. Tech­
niques such as multi-wavelet transformations and data-driven feature
extraction have been shown to improve noise suppression and signal
characterization in electrophysiological recordings [4,17]. For example,
wavelet-based approaches using Ricker, Gaussian, and Morlet functions
have achieved high classification accuracy by capturing complementary
temporal–spectral characteristics of ERG signals [18]. These de­
velopments have facilitated improved detection of pathological patterns
across a wide range of retinal and optic nerve disorders, including
glaucoma, retinitis pigmentosa, and less common inflammatory and
ischemic conditions [4].
Beyond ophthalmology, structured signal decomposition and
feature-engineering frameworks have proven effective across multiple
understanding biomedical domains. Empirical Wavelet Transform
(EWT), Fourier–Bessel series expansion, and multivariate decomposition
techniques have been successfully applied to electrocardiographic
(ECG), electroencephalographic (EEG), and pulmonary sound signals for
automated disease detection, including myocardial infarction, bundle
branch block, epilepsy, and respiratory disorders [19–21]. These studies
demonstrate that physiologically motivated band-wise decomposition,
followed by interpretable feature extraction, can yield robust and clin­
ically meaningful diagnostic models. Similar wavelet-based approaches
have also been applied to ophthalmic imaging, such as automated
glaucoma detection from fundus images, highlighting the broad appli­
cability of multiscale signal analysis in biomedical diagnostics [22].
Pattern electroretinography (PERG) has consequently emerged as a
critical tool for assessing macular and retinal ganglion cell function,
particularly in distinguishing macular diseases from optic nerve disor­
ders [23,24]. Recent advancements in signal processing and machine
learning (ML) have expanded its diagnostic potential. For instance,
Canedo et al. proposed novel modeling approaches for PERG signal
analysis, leveraging wavelet transforms and time-frequency decompo­
sition to enhance feature extraction for retinal pathology detection [25].
Similarly, studies such as Asanad and Karanjia emphasize PERG's clin­
ical applicability in diagnosing conditions such as glaucoma and
inherited maculopathies [23], while Holder et al. linked PERG abnor­
malities to fundus autofluorescence patterns in macular dystrophies
[24]. The ISCEV standards (Bach et al., 2013) provide a standard pro­
cedure for consistent PERG acquisition, though gaps remain in trans­
lating these protocols into automated, multi-disease classification
systems [15,26].
Despite these advances, most existing machine learning studies focus
on binary classification tasks (e.g., glaucoma versus healthy controls)
using ERG or OCT data, with relatively limited attention given to PERGbased multi-pathology classification [27]. Moreover, while
wavelet-based and spectral features have demonstrated promise in ERG
analysis, systematic evaluation of band-limited PERG signals for dis­
tinguishing diverse retinal and optic nerve disorders remains underex­
plored [28,29]. While prior studies have applied machine learning to
diagnose individual retinal diseases, the majority rely on binary classi­
fication paradigms and limited datasets [30], To address these limita­
tions, this study utilizes the IOBA-PERG dataset that contains a
comprehensive repository of 1354 transient pattern electroretinography
(PERG) responses, to address broader diagnostic challenges through two
key approaches [4]. Advanced signal processing techniques are applied
to analyze waveform morphology, focusing on features such as
N35–P50–N95 latencies, oscillatory potentials, and spectral character­
istics. Drawing from established EEG signal processing method­
ologies—where frequency band segregation and band-pass filtering
(0.5–100 Hz) have enabled robust feature extraction [31], this study

adopts a band limited PERG analysis framework to enhance interpret­
ability. The PERG signals are segmented into five physiologically
motivated frequency bands:
1. Very-low (0–1 Hz): Captures slow oscillatory components associated
with metabolic retinal processes
2. Low (1–5 Hz): Isolates the N95 wave, indicative of inner retinal
dysfunction
3. Mid (5–15 Hz): Emphasizes the P50 wave, representing macular
function
4. High (15–30 Hz): Associated with transient glaucomatous responses
5. Very-high (30–50 Hz): May encode high-frequency oscillations in
ischemic retinopathies
Unlike prior ERG and PERG studies that primarily apply spectral
filtering as a preprocessing step or focus on isolated disease detection,
the present work introduces a structured, band-wise diagnostic frame­
work in which frequency-specific PERG components are independently
analyzed, statistically validated, and systematically compared across
multiple retinal and optic nerve disorders. The novelty therefore lies not
in the use of standard band-pass filters, but in the diagnostic interpre­
tation and comparative evaluation of band-limited PERG features across
diverse pathologies.
Second, multi-class classification models are developed to distin­
guish among 15 retinal pathologies using time-domain features derived
from band-limited PERG signals. The primary objective is to establish a
machine learning–driven framework that incorporates frequencyoptimized PERG features for the diagnosis of retinal disorders,
including early-stage glaucoma, diabetic macular edema, and retinitis
pigmentosa. By systematically evaluating the diagnostic relevance of
predefined frequency bands, this study aims to address existing gaps in
PERG-based classification and to highlight the advantages of frequencyadaptive filtering over conventional time-domain analysis.
Although existing literature confirms the clinical relevance of PERG
signals for assessing retinal function, significant gaps remain in their
analytical use for automated classification and early detection. In
particular, frequency-domain analysis of PERG signals has received
limited attention in machine learning–based ophthalmic diagnostics.
This work addresses this gap by investigating the diagnostic value of
PERG frequency components using an ML architecture trained on the
open-access IOBA-PERG dataset from PhysioNet. The proposed frame­
work, illustrated in Fig. 1, analyzes multi-pathology PERG responses,
with implementation details and results presented in the subsequent
sections.
2. Methodology
This section outlines the complete methodology adopted in this
study, including signal preprocessing, band segmentation, feature
extraction, and the feature validation. Each process is described in detail
in the following subsections.
2.1. Dataset description
In this research, the publicly available IOBA-PERG dataset from
PhysioNet [4] have been used, which comprises 1,354 transient pattern
electroretinogram (PERG) responses recorded from 304 individuals,
encompassing a total of 336 independent session records. Each record
contains bilateral retinal signals sampled at a rate of 170 Hz, alongside
extensive clinical metadata including age, sex, and multiple diagnostic
labels (diagnosis1, diagnosis2, diagnosis3). In this analysis, only the
primary diagnostic field (diagnosis1) was used to define the ground
truth labels for supervised multi-class classification system.
Although the complete dataset includes a broad range of retinal and
optic nerve pathologies, a curated subset consisting of 15 clinically
significant disease categories, including healthy controls, have been

2.5. ANOVA-Based feature evaluation for PERG signal discrimination
Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was employed in this study exclu­
sively as a post-hoc statistical analysis tool to support physiological
interpretation of the extracted PERG features. Importantly, ANOVA was
not used for feature selection, dimensionality reduction, or model
optimization, and it did not influence the training or testing of the
machine learning classifiers. All extracted features were retained and
provided as input to the classification models.
The primary objective of the ANOVA analysis was to assess the sta­
tistical relevance of individual features in differentiating retinal pa­
thology classes and to provide insight into which frequency bands and
signal characteristics exhibit stronger group-level separation. One-way
ANOVA is well suited for such exploratory analysis in multi-class
biomedical datasets, as it compares between-class variance against
within-class variance to quantify feature sensitivity across diagnostic
categories.

3. Machine learning models for retinal pathology classification
Machine learning models were developed using feature sets extrac­
ted from PERG signals recorded from both the left and right eyes of each
subject. Time-domain features were derived from three predefined fre­
quency bands (1–5 Hz, 5–15 Hz, and 15–30 Hz) independently for each
eye, forming the input feature vectors for classification. For each subject,
a total of 66 features were extracted and associated with a corresponding
diagnostic class label. The resulting feature vectors were organized into
a consolidated table structure, with each row representing a unique trial
indexed by a generated SubjectID.
The original dataset comprised 15 diagnostic classes (class num ∈ {1,
…, 15}), each corresponding to a distinct retinal condition. To compre­
hensively evaluate the classification capability of the extracted PERG
features, three classification strategies were implemented:
(i) binary classification to distinguish normal from pathological
cases,
(ii) multi-class classification using all 15 original labels, and
(iii) five-class multi-class classification based on clinically motivated
regrouping of related conditions.
The five-class grouping consisted of Normal signals (106 records),
Retinitis Pigmentosa (47 records), Macular Dystrophy (33 records),
Stargardt Disease (16 records), and a consolidated category comprising
related retinal and optic nerve disorders with overlapping PERG
response characteristics. This regrouping was guided primarily by
shared pathophysiological mechanisms and electrophysiological simi­
larities observed in PERG recordings, with class imbalance considered as
a secondary factor to support stable classifier training. All evaluations
were performed using record-level random splits; subject-wise and
cross-dataset validation were not applied and are reserved for future
studies. The results corresponding to each classification strategy are
presented and discussed in the subsequent sections.

4.5. Eye-Region and high-frequency feature interpretation
In contrast, features derived from higher frequency bands (15–30
Hz) were more effective in capturing left–right eye differences. Highfrequency PERG components are typically associated with transient
neural responses and rapid signal fluctuations, which may be more
susceptible to subtle asymmetries arising from disease progression,
electrode placement, or inter-eye physiological variation. Features such
as skewness, kurtosis, and peak-to-peak amplitude in this band were
therefore more informative for within-subject comparisons rather than
cross-pathology discrimination. This complementary behavior high­
lights the importance of band-specific feature interpretation depending
on the diagnostic objective.

4.6. Clinical and methodological implications

The observed dominance of low-frequency PERG components (1–5
Hz) in pathology classification can be attributed to their strong associ­
ation with sustained retinal ganglion cell (RGC) activity and macular
processing. Pathologies such as glaucoma, retinitis pigmentosa, and
macular dystrophies are known to alter slow, sustained neural responses
rather than transient high-frequency activity. Consequently, features

5. Conclusion
This study investigated frequency-band–specific feature behavior in
PERG signals for the classification of retinal and optic nerve pathologies.
Beyond numerical performance comparisons, the findings provide
insight into the physiological relevance of different spectral compo­
nents. In particular, low-frequency PERG bands demonstrated stronger
discriminative capability across pathology classes, which may be
attributed to their association with global retinal ganglion cell responses
and sustained neural activity. In contrast, higher-frequency features
showed greater sensitivity to inter-eye variability, suggesting their po­
tential role in capturing localized or asymmetrical functional changes.
The structured band-wise analysis employed in this work enables
systematic comparison of feature relevance across disorders, rather than
treating spectral analysis solely as a preprocessing or noise-reduction
step. By decomposing the PERG signal into diagnostically meaningful
frequency bands and analyzing each band independently, the proposed
framework facilitates clearer interpretation of how different spectral
